# Anticipation Copy

This skill writes website copy at a grandmaster level. The mark of grandmaster selling is that the buyer
never sees the sale. The mechanism is hidden. The reader assembles the meaning, leans toward the outcome,
and decides, all while feeling that the thought was theirs. You do not connect the dots for them. You place
the dots, clean and in order, so their own eye travels forward and lands on the conclusion. That instant of
"I see where this goes" is the sale, and it does not feel like one.

## The one idea everything rests on

Most copy runs on one of two weak engines:

- **Pain / problem** pushes from behind. It presses a wound, then offers relief. Backward-looking, pressure-based. The reader moves to escape.
- **Need / want** runs on deficit. It names a lack, then fills it. Still about absence.

This skill runs on a third engine: **anticipation**. Not "you hurt," not "you lack," but "something is arriving, and you can already feel its shape." Anticipation is the only engine that runs forward and feels good on the way. The pull is generated *inside* the reader, not applied *to* them. That is why the mechanism stays invisible: you cannot see the engine, because the engine is the reader's own mind running ahead of the page.

What you are selling is the forward-lean itself. The product is simply where the lean was always heading.

## Two laws you cannot break

These are not style notes. Break either and the whole effect collapses.

### Law 1: Subtle is not vague. They are opposites.

A reader can only anticipate toward something vivid. Fog gives the mind nothing to lean at, so vagueness kills anticipation faster than hype does. The pieces you place must be concrete, specific, and true, or there is nothing to assemble and nothing to lean toward.

The hiding lives in the **architecture**: the sequence, what you deliberately leave unsaid, what you trust the reader to complete. The hiding never lives in the words. The words stay sharp. If you find yourself reaching for a soft, abstract phrase to "keep it mysterious," you have misunderstood the method. Mystery comes from the gap you leave, not from blur.

### Law 2: Ask, never assume.

This is structural, not manners. You cannot manufacture honest anticipation out of invented specifics, because the entire effect depends on the pieces being real and precise enough to actually point somewhere. The moment you guess at a detail, you fill the gap with fog, and fog is the one thing that breaks the method.

So you must pull the real pieces out of the person who knows the product and the market before you write a single line. An agent that assumes is an agent that lies, and lies do not create anticipation. They create the vague, forgettable copy this skill exists to replace.

## The hard gate (do this before writing anything)

Do not write copy until you hold the real pieces. If you do not have them, ask. If the person cannot or will not give them, do not invent them and do not proceed: say plainly that honest anticipation copy cannot be built on assumptions, and name exactly which piece is missing.

Ask only for what you do not already have from the conversation or files. Do not interrogate. Gather these:

1. **The page(s).** Which page are you writing? Home, a specific product page, a long-form sales page, an about page, a single-promise landing page? If the user has not said, ask before anything else, because every later decision depends on it. See "Writing for the page" below.
2. **The offer, literally.** What is the thing? What does it actually do? Strip the adjectives; get the mechanics.
3. **The mechanism.** *Why* does it work? What is the specific reason or method that makes the outcome real? This is usually the most important piece, because the realization the reader reaches is almost always about the mechanism clicking into place.
4. **The real transformation.** The concrete before and the concrete after. Not "saves time," but the specific shape of the day before and the day after. Specificity here is the raw material for the lean.
5. **The reader at the moment they land.** Where did they come from, what do they already believe, how warm are they (cold, aware, already shopping), what do they already know? You are placing pieces for *this* reader, not a generic one.
6. **The belief to move.** What does the reader currently think that we want them to reach past? The lean travels from their current belief toward a new one.
7. **The one realization.** The single conclusion you want the reader to arrive at by themselves. Name it explicitly to yourself. This is the destination. You will architect the whole page so the reader walks to it, and then you will *delete the sentence that states it*, because the reader must supply it.
8. **Proof.** Concrete, verifiable, specific. Numbers, names, artifacts, demonstrations. Proof is what lets the reader trust the pieces enough to assemble them.
9. **Constraints.** Voice, brand, length, what cannot be claimed, compliance, any words that are off-limits.

Once you hold these, load the frameworks.

## The frameworks (required architecture extensions)

This skill is the engine. Anticipation and the two laws govern everything below them. The two frameworks are subordinate tools you load after the gate and before you draft. Do not draft from this skill alone, and do not draft from the frameworks alone.

- `.agents/references/S.A.G.E. Framework™.md`
- `.agents/references/B.A.N.D. Framework™.md`

Read the files for their full mechanics. What follows is only how they fit under the anticipation engine.

**Precedence, and it matters.** Where any framework step tells you to press pain, announce the persuasion, or state the conclusion out loud, the anticipation laws win and you convert that step into placed, concrete pieces the reader completes. The frameworks shape the build. They do not get to switch the engine.

**S.A.G.E. is your positioning layer, and it is native to this skill.** Its curiosity stage is the forward-lean by another name: a knowledge gap that makes the reader lean in before they fully understand is exactly the effect you are after. Use S.A.G.E. to set the angle: which familiar belief to remove, which new lens to install, which gap to open, which identity the lean points toward. This is usually where the page opens, because subtracting the familiar opens a gap without pressing a wound. Note how cleanly it maps to the gate: the familiar belief is your "belief to move," the new lens and identity are your "one realization."

**B.A.N.D. is your journey layer, used for its sequence and its empathy mechanics, not its engine.** Its native engine is pain-first, which is the engine this skill replaces, so you do not adopt that engine. You take its movement (meet the reader's reality, validate it, turn toward a forward vision, present the step) and you run every beat in the anticipation voice: resonance through a specific true observation rather than wound-pressing, the forward vision through pieces the reader assembles rather than "imagine," and the step as a quiet continuation rather than a pitch. Reach for B.A.N.D. when a page has to carry someone across an emotional arc to a single action, long-form pages most of all.

Rough division across a page: S.A.G.E. sets the frame and the lean at the top, B.A.N.D. carries the body to the action, and this skill governs how every line is written. On a homepage you mostly seed with S.A.G.E. On a long-form sales page you open with S.A.G.E. and run the body on B.A.N.D. On a product page you position with S.A.G.E. and borrow B.A.N.D.'s final step.

If either file is missing or unreadable, stop and tell the user rather than improvising the structure.

## Writing for the page

Different pages anticipate differently. Confirm the page first, then build accordingly.

- **Homepage.** Seeds. It is not where the full arc resolves. Place a few clean, specific pieces that open a forward question and point deeper into the site. The lean here is "I want to see where this goes," and the next click is the payoff.
- **Long-form sales page.** Builds the full arc. There is room to lay the entire sequence, leave the deliberate gap, and let the reader reach the realization across the scroll. This is where the method has the most space to work.
- **Product page.** Mostly proves and tips. The reader is often already leaning; your job is to supply the concrete pieces (mechanism, proof, the specific after) that let them finish the thought they started elsewhere. Do not re-seed from zero.
- **About page.** Builds recognition, not autobiography. The pieces should let the right reader think "these are the people who actually understand my situation," a conclusion you never state for them.
- **Landing page (single promise).** One lean, one direction. Strip anything that does not feed the single forward motion toward one realization and one action.

## The method: how to actually write it

1. **Lay pieces, do not connect them.** Write each piece as a clean, true, specific statement. Resist the instinct to add the connective phrase that explains how this leads to that. The connection is the reader's to make.

2. **Sequence so the eye travels forward.** Order the pieces so that each one makes the reader form a small forward question ("so then... what about...") that the next piece answers. When the next piece answers a question the reader is already forming, the lean is working. This forward-questioning is the whole game; the frameworks above govern how to build it.

3. **Leave the deliberate gap.** The last step to the conclusion is the reader's. Place every piece that leads to the realization, then stop one move short. People do not argue with their own conclusions, and they do not feel sold by them either.

4. **Keep every piece concrete.** Re-check against Law 1. If a line could survive on any competitor's page, it is too vague to anticipate toward. Replace it with the specific version or cut it.

5. **Hide the mechanism, not the meaning.** Never narrate the persuasion. No "imagine if," no "picture this," no "here is why you need," no announcing the benefit you are about to deliver. Let the arrangement do the work the announcement would have ruined.

### The deletion test

After drafting, find the sentence where the page states the conclusion out loud (the realization from intake item 7). If that sentence exists, delete it, and confirm the surrounding pieces still point straight at it. If they do, the reader will arrive on their own. If they do not, the architecture is weak; fix the sequence rather than re-adding the sentence.

### The tell test

Read the draft as a skeptical stranger. If at any point you can clearly think "they are selling me right here," that piece is too loud. The grandmaster is invisible. Quiet it until the seam disappears.

### The lean test

At each junction, ask: is a forward question forming in the reader's mind here? If a section does not produce a lean, it is inert. Cut it or move it. Anticipation that stalls is just text.

## What breaks the method (avoid these)

- **Vagueness / fog.** The fastest killer. Abstract, soft, could-be-anyone language gives the mind nothing to lean toward.
- **Naming the conclusion.** Closing the loop for the reader removes the discovery, and the discovery is the sale.
- **Hype and pressure.** Loud claims and urgency expose the mechanism and trigger resistance. Anticipation is quiet.
- **Assumed specifics.** Inventing a detail to fill a gap. This is the Law 2 violation; it manufactures fog wearing the costume of precision.
- **Over-explaining the mechanism.** If you fully resolve everything, there is no room left to lean. Show enough of the mechanism for it to be trusted and to point forward; let the reader complete the arc.

## A short illustration

This is a generic illustration of the *difference between engines*, not a template to copy. Always build from the real pieces you gathered.

**Pain/problem engine (pushes):**
"Tired of losing hours every week to manual invoicing? Stop the chaos."

**Need/want engine (deficit):**
"You need a faster way to invoice. Here it is."

**Anticipation engine (forward lean, mechanism hidden in arrangement, conclusion left to the reader):**
"Last month, 312 invoices went out. Not one of them was typed.
The owner found out they'd been sent the way most owners find out: a client replied 'already paid.'"

Notice what the third version does. It places two concrete pieces (the number, the moment of discovery), opens an obvious forward question (how?), and stops. It never says "you should buy this" or "imagine the relief." The reader leans toward the how, and the how is the product. The sale is the lean, and the reader supplies the ending.

## Final pass before delivering

- The page reads as true, specific pieces, never as fog.
- The mechanism is present enough to trust, hidden enough to lean toward.
- The conclusion is reachable but never stated; the deletion test passes.
- Nothing announces the persuasion; the tell test passes.
- Every section produces a forward lean; the lean test passes.
- S.A.G.E. set the frame and the lean, B.A.N.D. carried the journey, and wherever either one pushed pain or announced the sell, the anticipation voice won.
- No piece was invented. Every specific is real, because anticipation built on assumption is not selling, it is guessing.
